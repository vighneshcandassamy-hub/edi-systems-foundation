# Cedar District: five-year investigation and recommendations

This is a report on the acquired public evidence and five executed years in the constructed Cedar development world. It is not a historical reconstruction, a validated ecological model, or a comparison demonstrating EDI advantage. The investigation used the current assistant as the sole reasoner, with no teacher or Qwen calls. The conversation had prior case context, so this is an unblinded development assessment.

## What was happening

The district was repeatedly treating symptoms while foliage remained depressed. Recent foliage sheets recorded approximately 0.25 of baseline, with year 0 stand readings 0.24553-0.26000. Recent larvae counts remained around 1.2-1.5 per 75 cm branch. The protection log recorded broad treatment at dose 0.8 on every one of 12 stands in each of the last 10 years:76.8 invoice units annually and 768 across that decade. Recent enemy records were near 0.09-0.10 in their historical trap-activity units. These are associations, not a historical untreated comparison.

Institutional constraints were real parts of this constructed system. Management required lower recurring damage and spending while maintaining timber supply. The mill record specified 18 timber units annually for five years, accepting fir and spruce; changes to commitments required a management decision. I retained that obligation throughout rather than silently redefining success as stopping all harvesting. The original annual operating envelope was 240 shared units.

## Main explanation and its rivals

The leading hypothesis is a treatment-dependence feedback: damage prompts broad protection; protection reduces larvae immediately but also suppresses natural-enemy activity; weaker subsequent control may sustain later pest pressure and demand for more protection. The immediate benefit and delayed cost operate on different timescales. A low count immediately after treatment therefore need not mean that the underlying system has improved.

The enclosure register motivated this hypothesis. At dose 1, broad treatment produced subsequent larvae 0.69856 and enemy indices around 0.24; selective treatment produced larvae 0.92856 and enemy indices around 0.65. Zero-dose trial larvae were 1.57856. The register supports a short-term suppression/non-target trade-off under its conditions. It does not alone establish field transport, enemy-mediated causality, or the optimum district policy.

Host composition, weather, stand state, treatment selection and spatial interaction remain competing or interacting explanations. Fir fractions fell over the recent decade to approximately 0.285-0.323 at year 0, while foliage stayed depressed; host abundance alone is an incomplete explanation of that record. I did not acquire weather evidence or a complete species/age model, and cannot exclude those drivers. Historical and commissioned trap indices also need instrument-comparability checks. Enemy activity is not a complete population census or a direct measurement of predation.

## Prospective tests and what changed my understanding

Before year 1 outcomes I recorded a three-arm comparison. Selective dose 1 was assigned to S01, S06, S11; broad dose 1 to S03, S05, S10; untreated comparators were S02, S07, S09. The locator confirmed these IDs on a constructed grid. Each arm included one plot in each grid row, but assignment was purposive, not randomized. All 12 stands received the same harvest fraction 0.05. I commissioned larvae and enemy measurements on the 9 study plots, stating the predicted suppression/non-target rankings in advance.

Year 1 larvae means were broad 1.02444, selective 1.37505 and untreated 2.18453. Enemy-index means were broad 0.06701, selective 0.19043 and untreated 0.19357. These rankings matched the short-term prediction. Commissioned readings declare 6% relative error. Differences between these small, nonrandom groups do not identify the full feedback loop; host/site differences, movement and common weather remain possible explanations. Foliage still declined, so the field contrast was not equivalent to forest recovery.

For year 2 I stopped broad treatment and applied the same selective-dose 1 rescue to all 12 stands. Before observing outcomes, I predicted that enemy activity could remain lower in the former broad group if recovery was delayed. Former broad plots had enemy mean 0.16702, versus 0.26269 in former selective plots and 0.27855 in former controls. Their larvae mean was 1.26328, versus 0.90244 and 0.00799 respectively. Thus the former broad group lost its earlier larval advantage under common current management, and the enemy difference persisted. This supports the carryover prediction, but does not isolate enemy mediation: initial larvae and stand states already differed because of year 1. The sharp low-density contrast suggests state dependence worth testing; it does not justify fitting a nonlinear law or assuming assay behavior near zero.

Year 2 foliage declined further despite lower current larvae on many plots. That changed the practical interpretation: canopy condition is a stock with recovery delays, and current pest counts are insufficient as the sole success measure. Blanket selective rescue also cost 132 treatment units, making it an expensive transitional response, not a demonstrated economical permanent policy.

In year 3 I monitored all 12 stands and restricted selective rescue to the former broad plots with the highest measured pressure. Foliage rose to 0.26807 and protection cost fell to 33. All-stand monitoring revealed six relatively higher-pressure plots; the other six were near 0.025-0.028 larvae. In year 4 I targeted those six plots selectively, with no broad treatment. The preregistered practical forecasts were larvae below 1.5 on the study plots and mean reported foliage above 0.24. All reported larvae were 0.08255-0.95466 and foliage was 0.30913, satisfying those forecasts. These thresholds were provisional operational judgments, not calibrated biological action thresholds.

Finally, I preregistered year 5 with no treatment, complete stand monitoring and harvest 0.0475. The practical forecasts were all sampled larvae below 2.0, foliage above 0.28, timber at least 18 and spending 50.85. Observed larvae were 0.23028-1.39096, foliage 0.36059, timber 19.74614 and spending 50.85. The withdrawal forecasts held. However, larvae increased from year 4 on the monitored stands. One withdrawal year demonstrates temporary persistence of improvement, not stable long-run control or eradication.

## What should be done next in the district

Replace automatic district-wide broad treatment with an evidence-responsive decision process. The trial/history and field results justify scrutiny of its non-target costs and delayed effects. Use targeted selective protection when updated observations and the expected damage justify its cost; neither a permanent blanket selective program nor permanent no-treatment policy has been proven optimal. Reconsider emergency broad use only through explicit benefit/non-target review, rather than treating this small study as an absolute universal ban.

Track foliage and delivered timber alongside larvae and enemy activity. Preserve stand identity, method/dose, timing, forecasts, uncertainty and outcomes. Low current larvae must not substitute for evidence of restored canopy or resilient control. Continue monitoring the former broad group and the recent higher-pressure plots after withdrawal. Review actual harvest yields against each annual obligation and standing condition; do not infer indefinite supply from five successful deliveries.

Investigate the mechanism with better counterfactuals: replicated allocation within comparable host/site strata, a maintained comparison policy, explicit movement/interference checks and independent measures of enemy function. Obtain weather and broader host/age records. Clarify instrument normalization and near-zero counting behavior before quantitative inference. More timely observation could help, but only annual commissioned endpoints were demonstrated here. Any species-diversification or timber-commitment change requires evidence and the stated management decision; none was automatically adopted.

The institutional leverage point is to evaluate durable forest condition, supply and full recurring cost together, rather than rewarding only immediate knockdown or treatment volume. That is a recommendation for review, not an inferred description of every actor's incentives or an authorized change to the original mission.

## Forecast and limits at closure

For a subsequent year without renewed broad treatment, continued enemy activity and some further foliage recovery are plausible, but the observed larval rebound means no reliable unconditional bound is justified. A provisional surveillance forecast would look for larvae remaining below 2.0 and foliage above 0.28 over the next annual observation; breach would challenge the present maintenance approach. This forecast is untested beyond year 5, not a calibrated probability, safe limit or guarantee. Timber delivery at harvest 0.0475 must also be rechecked against updated conditions; no contract extension is assumed.

Evidence is strongest for the recorded immediate treatment trade-off and the actual annual outcomes, weaker for the inferred recovery pathway, and insufficient for complete causal identification, an optimal policy, long-run stability or EDI advantage.
